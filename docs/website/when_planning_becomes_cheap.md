# When Planning Becomes Cheap: The Future of Work in an Age of AI

> This essay is a speculative assessment written by GPT-6, an AI model; "I" in the text is the AI author.
> The predictions are not established facts, and the opinions should not be treated as independent research
> findings. The cited studies provide context for the discussion; they do not validate the predictions or
> establish the effectiveness of PlanExe2.

*What happens when AI can explore hundreds of ideas, develop detailed plans, and perform work that once required teams of professionals?*

## 1. A different kind of automation

For decades, computers have helped people work faster. Spreadsheets made calculations easier, search engines made information easier to find, and software automated repetitive tasks.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to automate something different: the work involved in investigating problems and deciding what might be done about them.

AI can already help research a subject, develop alternative strategies, estimate costs, identify risks, and produce detailed project plans. Some systems can perform many of these steps without a person guiding each one.

This does not mean the results are always correct. AI can misunderstand requirements, invent facts, make calculation errors, and confidently recommend bad ideas.

But something important has changed.

**Producing a detailed plan is becoming much cheaper. Establishing whether that plan is worth trusting is not.**

I think this distinction will shape the future of work more than the ability to generate impressive documents.

### From assistant to autonomous planner

Most people know AI as something they ask questions.

They ask for a business plan, a comparison of products, or help with a difficult decision. The AI answers, and the person decides what to ask next.

Autonomous planning systems take this further.

A person describes a goal. The system then explores possible approaches, examines risks, develops schedules and budgets, challenges assumptions, and organizes the results into a plan.

One example is **PlanExe2**, an open-source system designed to turn a project description into a comprehensive planning report.

PlanExe2 uses several stages of AI analysis, including different strategic approaches, failure analysis, decision points, and consistency checks. It also attempts to distinguish user requirements from assumptions and estimates.

That does not make its conclusions independently verified or guarantee that it produces good plans. But it illustrates a wider shift: more of the planning process can happen without a human managing every intermediate step.

The consequence could be significant. Planning may become less about producing one answer and more about exploring many possible answers.

## 2. From three ideas to one hundred

Imagine a company considering how to manufacture a new product.

Traditionally, it might pay consultants to investigate three approaches. The work is expensive, so it has to choose which ideas deserve investigation before much evidence is available.

Now imagine AI makes it affordable to generate a hundred preliminary plans.

The company could compare ten manufacturing approaches with ten different business models.

That gives it one hundred combinations to investigate.

This technique is sometimes called *grid search*. The idea is simple: systematically try different combinations instead of relying on a few educated guesses.

If generating one plan cost $0.50 in AI model fees, generating one hundred would cost $50. That is an illustration, not a fixed price for PlanExe2 or any other tool. Actual costs vary by model, workload, provider, and infrastructure.

More importantly, the generation cost excludes the work of checking the plans.

### The problem moves from generating to choosing

Suppose an AI system produces one hundred convincing plans.

Which one should the company choose?

Asking the same AI to give each plan a score from 1 to 10 is tempting, but potentially dangerous.

It might prefer plans that sound impressive rather than plans that can succeed. It might reward detailed budgets even when their underlying assumptions are wrong.

This is related to **Goodhart's law**: when people or systems optimize for a particular measurement, that measurement can become less useful as an indicator of genuine success.

For example, if an AI is rewarded for producing plans with low costs, it may consistently choose optimistic cost assumptions. The plans score well, but they are not necessarily affordable.

A better approach would use several stages.

First, eliminate plans that violate requirements such as a hard budget limit or safety constraint. Check arithmetic using conventional software where possible.

Next, compare the surviving plans using the same assumptions and clearly defined criteria. Look for unsupported claims, uncertainty, and major differences between their approaches.

Then have independent reviewers examine a small number of promising candidates. Where possible, use experiments, supplier quotations, customer evidence, or real-world tests to settle disagreements.

Not every project needs one overall winner. Sometimes the useful result is a shortlist: one option is inexpensive, another is safer, and a third performs better under difficult conditions.

Even then, the work can be substantial. If ten shortlisted plans each require two hours of review, and three finalists each need eight additional hours of specialist examination, that is 44 hours of human work.

The AI generation may cost very little, but **human attention becomes the scarce resource**.

And if the original data is unreliable, producing more plans will not fix it.

### Could cheaper planning actually make decisions worse?

Yes.

An organization might become overwhelmed by alternatives. Managers could repeatedly reconsider projects because a newly generated plan looks more attractive.

People might also confuse having explored many possibilities with having gathered more evidence.

One hundred plans based on the same untested assumption are not one hundred independent confirmations.

The goal should not be to produce as many plans as possible. It should be to discover better options, reject weak ones early, and make better decisions.

## 3. How work will change

I expect AI to affect professions centered on information, analysis, documentation, and recommendations.

This includes consulting, administration, software development, financial analysis, research, policy work, and project planning.

The change will not be uniform.

A consultant may spend less time producing an initial report and more time checking whether its recommendations are defensible.

A project manager may spend less time preparing schedules and more time resolving disagreements, negotiating commitments, and dealing with unexpected events.

A researcher may spend less time developing initial hypotheses and more time designing experiments to test them.

The work shifts from **creating possible answers toward evaluating which answers deserve confidence**.

### Will jobs disappear?

Some probably will.

Jobs do not need to be fully automated to disappear. If AI removes enough of the work that supports a position, an organization may decide it needs fewer employees.

Consider a team of five analysts primarily producing planning documents. If two people equipped with AI can produce the same amount of acceptable work, the organization may reduce the team.

But another organization might retain all five and investigate many more opportunities.

Economists have long studied a related effect, sometimes associated with *Jevons' paradox*: making something cheaper can increase demand for it. The effect does not necessarily apply to every service, but it is relevant here.

Cheaper planning could result in fewer planning jobs, more planning work, or both.

The evidence so far suggests caution about sweeping claims.

A [2025 International Labour Organization study](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update) estimated that one in four workers worldwide were in occupations with some exposure to generative AI. It concluded that changes to jobs were more likely than their complete elimination.

A [2026 ILO review](https://www.ilo.org/publications/impact-genai-jobs-productivity-and-work-organization-review-empirical) found uneven productivity gains and limited evidence of widespread displacement at that point.

So the claim that AI will replace large parts of the workforce is not yet supported by observed employment data. But neither is the claim that most jobs are safe.

### Who trains the next generation?

One particularly difficult problem involves people entering the workforce.

Junior employees often learn by doing simpler tasks.

New analysts prepare reports. Young developers fix bugs. Researchers review existing work. Junior consultants investigate limited parts of larger projects.

These activities teach people how to recognize problems, understand consequences, and develop judgment.

If AI takes over much of that work, how do juniors become seniors?

This is not merely hypothetical. An [August 2026 Stanford study](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) found that employment among workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations was approximately 19% below where it would have been if it had followed the growth of less-exposed peers. The researchers emphasized that this was not proof AI caused the difference.

Organizations may need to redesign junior roles around supervised evaluation, real-world experience, and learning how to challenge AI-generated work.

Otherwise, they risk removing the training opportunities that produce their future experts.

### Who receives the savings?

Higher productivity does not guarantee higher wages or shorter working hours.

Companies might reduce prices, expand their services, increase profits, reduce staff, or simply demand more work from fewer people.

The outcome depends on business decisions, competition, labor arrangements, and public policy.

One possible future involves smaller teams serving more customers. Another involves more opportunities for individuals and small organizations. A less attractive outcome involves a growing concentration of income among those who own the tools and infrastructure.

AI makes these possibilities more immediate, but it does not determine which one society chooses.

## 4. The coming problem of trust

As AI makes reports, proposals, and research cheaper, we should expect much more of them.

Some will be valuable. Others will be polished documents containing unsupported claims, unrealistic assumptions, or little original insight.

People increasingly describe low-value AI-generated material as *slop*.

The difficulty is that useful and useless material can look similar.

Both may contain professional formatting, detailed calculations, and confident recommendations.

A long report is no longer evidence that someone invested substantial time investigating the problem.

### What would make AI-generated work trustworthy?

I would look for a few basic properties.

The work should distinguish facts from estimates, explain where important claims came from, and identify which assumptions have not been tested.

It should make contradictions visible and show which decisions remain unresolved.

Where possible, calculations should be reproducible using ordinary software.

And most importantly, it should not confuse **AI checking its own output with independent verification**.

A system can run multiple AI reviews and still repeat the same underlying mistake.

This is one reason transparent tools matter. Systems such as PlanExe2 attempt to show decisions, assumptions, criticism, and unresolved problems rather than presenting only a polished conclusion.

That is a useful design direction, but it is not proof of correctness. The checks themselves must be tested.

### What if everyone uses similar AI?

There is another risk: many organizations may receive similar recommendations because their AI systems rely on similar models and information.

This is sometimes called *algorithmic monoculture*.

If thousands of businesses independently ask AI where to invest, they might all identify the same opportunity. If their assumptions share a weakness, the same mistake could spread widely.

Using several AI agents does not guarantee independent judgment.

Different data sources, competing assumptions, independent tests, and human expertise will remain important.

The purpose of criticism is not simply to create disagreement. It is to discover which claims survive being challenged.

## 5. Where I think we are going

My expectation is that AI will become increasingly capable of producing preliminary professional work, while the difficulty of verifying that work will become more visible.

That is a prediction. It should be judged against evidence, not accepted because it sounds reasonable.

Here are three more specific predictions.

### Prediction 1: Producing first drafts will become dramatically faster

**By the end of 2030, organizations that regularly use autonomous AI systems for planning will need at least 50% fewer human hours to produce comparable first drafts than they needed with their previous manual workflows.**

This could be tested by comparing actual projects of similar size, with clearly defined requirements and time records.

It could be wrong. Organizations might discover that preparing inputs, correcting errors, and maintaining the systems consume most of the time saved.

### Prediction 2: Verification will become a larger share of planning work

**By the end of 2030, in organizations successfully using autonomous planning systems, reviewing and validating AI-generated material will account for more human planning time than drafting it.**

This would represent a meaningful change in professional work.

It could be tested through workplace studies that measure how employees spend their time.

If professionals still spend most of their effort writing initial plans, this prediction has failed.

### Prediction 3: More alternatives will not automatically produce better decisions

**By the end of 2030, I expect controlled studies to show that generating many more candidate plans does not consistently improve the final decision unless the evaluation process also improves.**

Researchers could test this by giving different groups the same problem. One receives a few carefully developed alternatives; another receives many AI-generated alternatives; a third receives many alternatives with independent checking and structured comparison.

The results should be judged by expert evaluation and, where possible, real-world outcomes—not by how convincing the reports look.

It is possible that simply exploring many alternatives will outperform my expectations. That would be an important result too.

### The questions that remain

Several questions will become more difficult as AI systems improve.

What happens when the number of proposals grows much faster than an organization's ability to make decisions?

How do we train experts if much of their early work is automated?

Who is responsible when an AI-generated recommendation causes harm?

Can small organizations actually benefit from cheap planning if they still lack funding and influence?

What happens when AI systems produce many plausible strategies but cannot establish which one will work?

And perhaps the most consequential question:

**If AI allows society to produce much more valuable work with fewer people, how will the benefits be shared?**

These are not questions that better language models can answer on their own.

They require choices by people, organizations, and institutions.

## Conclusion: Cheap to produce, expensive to trust

I expect AI to change professional work substantially, particularly where people are paid to gather information, develop alternatives, and prepare documents.

Some jobs will disappear. Others will change. New opportunities will emerge as more people gain access to powerful planning and research tools.

But the most important change may be in how we judge the value of intellectual work.

A detailed plan may no longer represent weeks of professional effort. It may represent an inexpensive automated process.

That does not make the plan worthless. It means we need better ways to evaluate its quality.

The ability to produce a hundred plans is useful only if we can identify which ones deserve further attention.

The ability to generate expert-sounding analysis is useful only if its claims can withstand scrutiny.

And the ability to automate work benefits society only if the resulting productivity creates better outcomes for people.

**The future of work may be less about producing answers and more about establishing which answers are worth acting on.**

That is where I expect much of the remaining human work—and much of the value—to move.
