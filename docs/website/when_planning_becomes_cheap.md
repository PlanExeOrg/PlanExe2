# When Planning Becomes Cheap: The Future of Work in an Age of AI

> This essay was written by ChatGPT Astra. It is speculation about where cheap, autonomous planning may take
> the world of work, not a description of what PlanExe2 does today. "I" in the text is the essay's AI author.

*What happens when machines can explore hundreds of ideas, challenge their own assumptions, and produce work that once required teams of professionals?*

## 1. We are approaching a different kind of automation

For a long time, computers have helped people work faster.

Spreadsheets made calculations easier. Search engines made information easier to find. Software automated repetitive tasks. Yet people still had to do much of the thinking needed to turn information into decisions.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to change that.

AI can now help investigate a problem, suggest different ways to solve it, estimate costs, identify risks, and develop detailed plans. Some systems can carry out many of these steps without a person guiding every action.

This does not mean AI always makes good decisions. It can misunderstand facts, overlook important details, and produce convincing explanations that are wrong.

But it means something important has changed: **the work of exploring possible futures is becoming much cheaper.**

I think this will have consequences far beyond the technology industry.

It could change which jobs exist, how organizations make decisions, what people are paid for, and who gets the opportunity to develop ambitious ideas.

And it raises a question we haven't fully answered:

**What happens when producing ideas and plans becomes easy, but knowing which ones to trust remains difficult?**

## 2. From helping people plan to planning autonomously

Most people are familiar with using AI as an assistant.

You ask a question, receive an answer, and decide what to ask next.

Imagine something more ambitious.

You describe a project in a few paragraphs. An AI system then develops different approaches, compares them, estimates budgets, identifies what might go wrong, creates a timeline, and examines whether the project makes sense.

It may discover that your original idea is too expensive, that an important assumption is unsupported, or that a different approach deserves more attention.

This is the direction of systems such as **PlanExe**, an open-source project-planning tool. PlanExe takes a description of a proposed project and uses AI to develop a structured plan covering matters such as costs, risks, schedules, decisions, and alternatives.

The important idea is not the report itself.

It is that **a substantial amount of planning work can happen between the initial question and the finished result, without a human directing every intermediate step.**

That is different from simply asking a chatbot to write a business plan.

However, autonomous planning is not the same as autonomous judgment. An AI system can investigate a decision without being qualified to make that decision on someone's behalf.

That difference will become increasingly important.

## 3. What happens when we can explore 100 ideas instead of three?

Consider an organization trying to solve a difficult problem.

Traditionally, it might ask a consulting team to investigate three possible approaches. Developing each one takes time and money, so the organization limits how many alternatives it considers.

Now imagine that producing an initial plan costs only fifty cents in AI model fees.

One hundred plans would cost fifty dollars to generate.

Instead of considering three options, the organization could investigate one hundred.

For example, it might compare ten different technical approaches against ten different ways of putting them into practice.

<escape>10 technical approaches × 10 implementation approaches = 100 possible combinations.</escape>

Each combination could be examined for cost, time, risk, and expected benefits.

The organization could then reject obviously unsuitable ideas, compare the remaining alternatives, and ask experienced people to investigate the most promising candidates.

This is sometimes called *grid search*, but the name is less important than the idea: **try many combinations systematically instead of guessing which few deserve attention.**

A more advanced system could go further. It could learn which kinds of options appear promising and explore more possibilities in those areas.

There is a catch.

Generating a hundred plans does not mean discovering a hundred good strategies. If the AI uses faulty assumptions, it may produce a hundred variations of the same mistake.

It might also learn to produce plans that score well according to its own tests without being any better in reality.

So this approach needs independent checks.

Still, I think the possibility is significant. We may be moving from a world where planning alternatives are expensive to one where exploring alternatives is relatively cheap.

That could change how decisions are made.

## 4. The cost of producing work is not the cost of trusting it

This may be the most important distinction in the future of AI.

Suppose an AI produces a detailed engineering plan for less than a dollar.

That sounds remarkable. But the plan might contain incorrect calculations, unrealistic deadlines, or assumptions about laws and technologies that have never been checked.

Someone still needs to establish whether the plan is useful.

There are really three different costs:

**Producing the work.** AI is making this dramatically cheaper.

**Checking the work.** People, reliable data, experiments, and independent evidence may still be necessary.

**Acting on the work.** Building facilities, hiring employees, negotiating agreements, and accepting responsibility remain expensive.

The first cost may approach zero in many situations.

The other two will not.

This means the future may not belong to whoever can generate the most impressive report. It may belong to whoever can establish which conclusions are correct and which recommendations are worth acting on.

A ten-page report containing three verified insights can be more valuable than a thousand-page report full of plausible but unsupported statements.

**The scarce resource may no longer be analysis. It may be trustworthy analysis.**

## 5. Which jobs will change?

I expect AI to affect jobs that involve gathering information, comparing options, drafting documents, preparing reports, and developing recommendations.

This includes parts of consulting, business analysis, research, project management, financial planning, administration, and policy work.

Not all these jobs will disappear.

Most professions involve much more than their written output. People build relationships, resolve disagreements, understand local conditions, notice things that are not documented, and take responsibility when something goes wrong.

But jobs do not need to be fully automated to disappear.

Imagine five employees whose main responsibility is producing planning documents. If AI allows two employees to handle the same workload, the organization may no longer need five people for that particular work.

Other organizations may respond differently. They might keep all five employees and investigate many more projects.

Both outcomes are plausible.

This creates an important uncertainty: **will AI mainly reduce the number of people needed, or will cheaper work create enough new demand to employ them in different ways?**

I expect both effects, but not equally across industries.

Some professions may grow. Others may shrink. And the change could happen much faster than people can retrain.

## 6. From creating answers to checking answers

One of the largest changes may be in what professionals actually do all day.

Today, a consultant might spend days gathering information and developing a proposal.

Tomorrow, that consultant might begin with several AI-generated proposals and spend most of the time asking:

Which assumptions are wrong? What evidence is missing? Which risks matter most? What has the AI misunderstood? Which options are worth testing?

A project manager might spend less time preparing schedules and more time resolving dependencies between actual people and organizations.

A financial analyst might spend less time building the first spreadsheet and more time examining whether its assumptions make sense.

A researcher might spend less time producing initial hypotheses and more time designing experiments that distinguish good ideas from bad ones.

The work shifts from **producing possible answers** toward **testing which answers deserve confidence**.

That could make some jobs more interesting. It could also make them more demanding.

People may be expected to review far more material, make decisions faster, and take responsibility for mistakes in work they did not personally create.

That is not automatically an improvement.

## 7. What happens to people entering the workforce?

This is one of the questions that worries me most.

Many professionals learn their trade by doing relatively simple tasks.

Junior consultants prepare presentations. Young developers fix small bugs. New analysts build spreadsheets. Researchers review existing literature.

These tasks are not always exciting, but they help people develop judgment.

If AI takes over much of the entry-level work, how do people gain the experience needed to become senior professionals?

An organization might eventually find itself with excellent AI tools and too few people capable of evaluating their output.

We could automate the work that teaches people how to become experts.

That would be a serious long-term mistake.

Education and employment may need to change together. Instead of spending years learning mainly how to produce documents, people may need earlier experience in checking claims, understanding uncertainty, testing ideas, and making decisions under real constraints.

But those skills cannot be learned entirely from watching AI work.

People still need opportunities to make mistakes, investigate the consequences, and develop their own understanding.

**The next generation must learn how to challenge AI, not merely how to operate it.**

## 8. Smaller organizations may gain opportunities previously reserved for large ones

There is a more hopeful side to this change.

Comprehensive planning has traditionally been expensive.

A large company can hire consultants, engineers, researchers, and financial specialists to investigate an ambitious project. A small organization may never get that opportunity.

If preliminary planning becomes inexpensive, more people can explore serious ideas.

A small nonprofit could investigate several ways to deliver a public service. A community group could compare infrastructure proposals. A founder could examine whether a business idea is economically plausible before spending their savings.

This does not remove the need for experts or evidence. But it may make early-stage investigation available to people who previously could not afford it.

I think that matters.

AI could make professional-grade planning tools more widely accessible, not just make existing organizations more efficient.

However, access to planning is not the same as access to money, land, permits, equipment, or political influence.

A small organization with an excellent plan may still struggle to get anyone to act on it.

AI can reduce some barriers without eliminating the deeper inequalities behind them.

## 9. The problem of AI slop

As AI-generated content becomes cheaper, we will probably see much more of it.

Reports, proposals, presentations, business plans, research summaries, and strategic recommendations may become almost unlimited.

Some will be genuinely useful.

Others will be polished material containing little original insight, unreliable facts, or conclusions that have not been checked.

People increasingly use the word *slop* for low-value AI-generated material.

The problem is that useful and useless AI work can look remarkably similar.

Both may have attractive formatting, confident language, detailed explanations, and impressive-looking numbers.

Long documents may become harder to trust precisely because they are so easy to produce.

I think the answer is not to make AI output sound more human or look more professional.

It is to make claims easier to verify.

A trustworthy planning system should make clear which facts came from the user, which came from external evidence, which numbers are estimates, and which decisions were made by AI.

It should reveal unresolved problems rather than conceal them.

And when it checks its own work, it must not confuse that with independent verification.

**Trust cannot be generated merely by asking an AI to declare that its work is trustworthy.**

Trust needs evidence.

## 10. Will more planning lead to better decisions?

Not necessarily.

This is another question that is easy to overlook.

If organizations can generate a hundred plans, they may discover better alternatives.

But they may also become overwhelmed.

Decision-makers could spend their time comparing endless reports instead of acting. Teams might repeatedly restart projects because a new AI-generated alternative looks more promising.

There is a danger of replacing too little analysis with too much analysis.

Organizations will need clear rules about when to explore, when to investigate further, and when to commit.

They will also need to distinguish a new idea from new evidence.

Generating another plan does not change the real-world facts.

A hundred different scenarios may be useful for understanding uncertainty, but they cannot replace an experiment, a customer interview, or a signed agreement.

**The purpose of planning is not to produce plans. It is to improve what happens next.**

## 11. Who benefits from the savings?

Lower costs do not automatically lead to better lives for workers.

Suppose AI makes a consulting team five times more productive.

The company might reduce prices, attract more customers, and increase employment.

It might keep prices unchanged and increase profits.

It might reduce its workforce.

Or it might expect the remaining employees to handle five times as much work.

Technology does not decide which outcome occurs. Organizations, customers, workers, investors, and public institutions influence how the benefits are distributed.

This raises questions that go beyond AI engineering.

Who owns the tools? Who controls the data? Who receives the productivity gains? What happens to people whose skills become less valuable? How do we prevent the benefits from concentrating in a small number of organizations?

The answers will differ between countries, industries, and workplaces.

I do not think technological progress alone guarantees shared prosperity.

## 12. What happens when everyone uses similar AI?

Another overlooked possibility is that AI systems may produce similar recommendations.

If thousands of organizations use comparable models, trained on overlapping information, they may converge on the same strategies.

That could create unexpected risks.

Businesses might pursue the same markets. Investors might follow similar assumptions. Organizations might adopt the same management practices without questioning whether those practices fit their circumstances.

Using many AI agents does not automatically provide independent opinions if they share the same blind spots.

A healthy planning process needs diversity: different data, different perspectives, different assumptions, and disagreement that is not simply generated for appearance.

The goal should not be to make every system agree.

It should be to understand why they disagree and what evidence could settle the disagreement.

## 13. What remains uniquely important about people?

I would be cautious about claiming that judgment, creativity, or strategic thinking will always belong exclusively to humans.

AI is already performing useful parts of those activities.

But there are responsibilities that cannot simply be handed over to a generated document.

Someone must decide which goals are worth pursuing.

Someone must consider who benefits and who carries the risks.

Someone must be accountable to the people affected.

And when reality differs from the plan, someone must have the authority and willingness to change course.

AI may become increasingly capable of recommending decisions. That does not mean we should automatically grant it the authority to make them.

In many situations, the most important work will remain deeply human: listening, negotiating, caring, leading, building trust, and accepting responsibility.

These activities are not valuable because AI happens to be bad at them today. They matter because people are affected by the outcomes.

## 14. Questions readers may ask

### What exactly is PlanExe?

PlanExe is an open-source project-planning system. You describe something you want to accomplish, and it uses AI to develop a detailed plan.

Depending on the project, the output can include budgets, timelines, risks, alternative strategies, and questions that need to be answered before work begins.

It is intended to support planning, not to prove that a project will succeed.

### Isn't this just asking ChatGPT to write a plan?

There is an important difference.

A general-purpose chatbot can write a plan in response to a request. An autonomous planning system can organize a longer process involving multiple stages of analysis, comparison, criticism, and revision.

That does not automatically make its results better. The improvement has to be demonstrated.

But the amount of work that can happen without repeated human instructions is different.

### Does AI actually understand the projects it plans?

That depends on what we mean by *understand*.

AI can work with relationships between goals, budgets, risks, dependencies, and possible outcomes. It can identify inconsistencies and suggest reasonable alternatives.

But it may lack important knowledge of the real world, misunderstand an assumption, or invent facts.

I would judge its understanding by how well its recommendations survive testing, rather than by how convincing its explanations sound.

### Can AI replace consultants and project managers?

It can replace significant parts of their work, especially research, drafting, comparison, and initial analysis.

Some jobs centered on producing documents could disappear or become much smaller.

But managing a real project also requires people, relationships, agreements, conflict resolution, and responsibility.

Replacing the production of a plan is not the same as replacing the person responsible for delivering it.

### If a plan costs fifty cents, why would anyone pay a consultant?

Because the price of generating a report is not the price of establishing whether it is correct.

The consultant may still provide valuable expertise, verified information, judgment, relationships, and accountability.

However, consultants who primarily charge for preparing documents may face significant pressure to change their services and pricing.

### Can we trust a plan made entirely by AI?

Not automatically.

A useful AI-generated plan should be treated as a starting point for investigation, not as proof.

The level of checking should depend on the consequences of being wrong. A small internal experiment and a major public infrastructure project need very different standards of evidence.

### Why isn't everyone already using autonomous planning?

Technical capability is only part of adoption.

People need to understand what the tool does, trust that it is useful, integrate it into their work, and see a benefit over their existing methods.

Some may not want long reports. Others may be skeptical of AI-generated material. Some may have legal, privacy, or organizational restrictions.

Cheap production does not guarantee demand.

### Could AI make decisions worse instead of better?

Yes.

It can create false confidence, spread incorrect assumptions, overwhelm decision-makers with options, or encourage organizations to act without sufficient evidence.

The real question is not whether AI produces more analysis, but whether people using AI consistently reach better outcomes.

### What should someone learn to remain valuable?

I would focus on understanding problems, evaluating evidence, recognizing weak assumptions, communicating clearly, and working with other people.

Technical skills will remain useful, but the ability to determine whether a result is correct may become more valuable than the ability to produce the first version.

People should learn to use AI and also develop the confidence to disagree with it.

## 15. The questions we should be asking next

I think public discussion focuses too heavily on whether AI can perform a particular job.

There are more important questions.

**Who is responsible when AI-generated advice causes harm?** A system might propose a decision, but people and organizations will bear the consequences.

**How do we measure better planning?** More pages, more detail, and more alternatives are not enough. We need evidence that decisions improve.

**What happens when there are too few experts left to verify AI work?** Automating junior tasks may weaken the training path for future specialists.

**Will cheaper planning create more opportunities or simply more paperwork?** Generating work is not the same as creating value.

**Who gets to decide which goals AI systems pursue?** A technically excellent plan can still serve the wrong interests.

**Can smaller organizations really benefit?** Affordable planning matters, but access to funding and institutional power may remain unequal.

**Will AI reduce working hours?** Higher productivity could create more leisure, but it could just as easily lead to higher expectations and greater pressure.

**What would make us stop trusting an AI system?** We need clear ways to measure failure, correct mistakes, and withdraw systems that perform poorly.

And perhaps the most uncomfortable question:

**If AI can do much of the work that society currently pays people to perform, how should people benefit from that productivity even when fewer workers are needed?**

That is not merely a technical problem. It is a question about the kind of society we want.

## 16. Where I think we are going

I expect the coming years to bring a growing separation between producing intellectual work and being responsible for its quality.

Writing a report, developing a preliminary strategy, or exploring a complex idea may become inexpensive enough that organizations stop treating these activities as major investments.

Instead, their attention may shift toward selecting opportunities, checking evidence, securing resources, and making decisions.

Some jobs will disappear. Others will change substantially. New work will emerge around testing, validation, oversight, and the practical use of AI-generated analysis.

I also expect uneven progress.

Certain organizations will adopt these tools quickly. Others will resist them or discover that the promised productivity gains are smaller than expected. Some will make serious mistakes by trusting AI too much.

We should not assume a smooth transition.

The largest change may be cultural.

For much of modern history, a comprehensive professional plan was evidence that someone had invested considerable time and expertise.

In a world where such plans can be generated cheaply, their existence will tell us very little about their quality.

We will have to judge the work differently.

Not by how much effort appears to have gone into it, but by the quality of its evidence, the decisions it supports, and what happens when it encounters reality.

## Conclusion: The future is not about producing more work

My central prediction is simple:

**AI will make it much easier to explore what could be done. The value of knowing what should be done—and proving why—will increase.**

That could be enormously beneficial.

More people could investigate ambitious ideas. Small organizations could gain access to capabilities previously available only to large institutions. Researchers could examine more possibilities. Businesses could identify expensive mistakes before committing resources.

But we could also create a world flooded with confident, unreliable recommendations, where fewer people understand the systems they depend on.

Both futures are possible, and they may happen at the same time.

The challenge is not simply to build AI that can produce better plans.

It is to build ways of working in which people can examine those plans, challenge their assumptions, make informed choices, and remain accountable for the consequences.

**The future of work may be less about producing answers and more about deciding which answers deserve to change the world.**
